# Multi-Year and Discount Strategy

_A reference the vendor-negotiation-support skill loads on demand, not a standalone policy
doc — treat the dollar figures and thresholds here as judgment aids and confirm the live
numbers in Apollo's systems._

Use this when the user is deciding term length, pushing for a bigger discount, responding
to a ramp-up proposal, evaluating use-or-lose credits, or deciding whether to renew or
reopen a competitive process. The concepts work for any vendor category — SaaS, data,
infrastructure, events, services. Read "budget owner" as whoever owns the spend (the
requesting manager, department head), and bring Finance in for larger or longer
commitments.

## Core Stance

- **1-year is the default.** A longer term is something Apollo *chooses to give* in
  exchange for real value, not a box the vendor gets to check.
- **Discount is negotiated, not tiered.** There is no fixed "X% for a 2-year term." The
  right discount is the vendor's floor — the point where they stop moving. Your job is to
  find it.
- **Every deal is different.** Spend, switching cost, competitive alternatives, category
  maturity, and timing all move the answer. Treat the rules below as judgment aids, not
  formulas. Confirm any dollar thresholds or co-sign requirements against Apollo's actual
  approval matrix in Zip — not a number in this doc.

## Getting the Discount: Ask, Then Keep Asking

The most reliable lever is persistence. Vendors quote a price expecting to give some of it
back. The discipline is to keep asking until there is no room left.

1. **Anchor before they do.** Open with your target, framed against usage or a competing
   option — not their list price. Never give your budget ceiling first.
1. **Treat their first number as the starting point.** The opening offer is rarely the
   floor. "Appreciate that — where can you get to if we sign this quarter / commit to the
   term / act as a reference?"
1. **Ask again.** "Is that your best?" costs nothing and moves price more often than not.
   Silence after their offer does the same work.
1. **Trade, never concede.** Every give should buy something: term length, a case study or
   logo rights, a faster signature, a referenceable expansion, better payment terms. Never
   hand over a longer commitment for free.
1. **Stop when they stop.** When two consecutive asks return "that's the best we can do" in
   the same words, you are at or near the floor. Bank it and move to terms (opt-out,
   rollover, escalation caps).

Discount and term are linked but not fungible on a fixed ratio. A longer term should always
earn a *deeper* discount and a price hold — but how much deeper is a negotiation, not a
lookup.

## Term Length: A Lever, Not a Formula

A longer term is worth it only when the value is real on all three:

- **Confidence the need persists** — proven production use and a defensible forward
  forecast, not a vendor projection.
- **Switching cost that has actually been quantified** — in writing, not asserted.
- **A discount and price hold big enough to matter** — a longer lock for a token discount
  is the worst of both worlds.

When you do go multi-year, never lock in without at least one escape valve. Get one or more
of:

- Annual opt-out or termination for convenience.
- Price protection: 0% escalation, or a cap tied to CPI.
- Usage rollover or true-down rights.
- A technology-refresh clause for fast-moving categories.

The bigger and longer the commitment, the more of these you should hold — and the higher up
the approval chain it goes. Loop in the budget owner for any multi-year, and Finance for the
largest or longest commitments; confirm the exact thresholds against Apollo's approval
matrix rather than assuming a cutoff.

**Fast-moving or immature categories (AI/ML, emerging infrastructure, new product bets):
stay at 1-year.** A short, flexible commit with no auto-renewal preserves a clean decision
point rather than betting that this year's competitive landscape holds two or three years
out. Prefer a fungible or flexible spend commit over a rigid lock, so budget can be
redirected as the category shifts. The same logic applies any time the market is moving
faster than the contract would.

**Side-by-side options:** When the owner has not settled on risk appetite, ask the vendor
for 1-, 2-, and 3-year pricing in one request and let the discount delta make the case.
Often the 2-year is the natural compromise — but only if the discount earns it.

## Ramp-Up Terms

A vendor-proposed ramp-up (Year 1 low, Years 2–3 materially higher) transfers risk to
Apollo: the vendor books committed revenue while easing its own Year 1 accountability.

**Default: reject vendor-shaped ramps. Counter with flat blended pricing** across the full
term.

Consider a ramp only when all of these hold:

1. Year 1 is at or below the current market rate — not a subsidized lock-in.
1. Increases are capped at CPI or a low single-digit percent, not the double-digit uplift
   vendors open with.
1. The ramp is **usage-based** — Apollo pays more because it uses more, not because the
   calendar turned.
1. The owner can put a written forecast behind the higher out-year minimums before signing.

**Walk-away:** if the Year 3 rate clears today's Year 1 market rate by a meaningful margin,
the ramp has eaten the multi-year discount. Go back to a 1-year deal.

Counter language:

```text
We'd prefer flat pricing across all years at the blended average of your proposal. If the
product delivers the value you're projecting, we'll expand naturally — but that expansion
should stay at the committed rate, not a higher out-year rate.
```

When you want the multi-year but not the vendor's ramp:

```text
We'll commit multi-year, but Year 2 and Year 3 minimums get set at 110% of Year 1 *actual*
spend, finalized at the end of Year 1 — not 110% of Year 1 contracted spend.
```

That puts performance accountability back on the vendor.

## Use-or-Lose Provisions

Credits, seats, or prepaid spend that expire within the year push Apollo to burn a product
just to avoid waste. That corrupts usage data, distorts cost-per-unit analysis, and hands
the vendor full-utilization optics at renewal even when the consumption was manufactured.

**Default: reject. Rollover is the floor you are willing to accept.**

- Ask for: "unused credits roll forward for 90 days into the next annual period." If they
  resist full carryforward, a rollover cap (for example, 25% of annual contract value) is
  the fallback.
- If they insist on hard expiry, **price the forfeiture in.** If Apollo typically uses ~80%
  of capacity, a hard use-or-lose clause means ~20% waste — so ask for a discount that
  offsets it. Note this only restores price parity on what you actually use; it does not
  also deliver the multi-year discount you would otherwise negotiate, so it is a worse
  outcome than rollover, not an equal one.
- Shrink the base commit toward actual usage — a smaller commit with expiry beats a large
  one with expiry.
- Require quarterly audit rights on usage reporting whenever expiry terms are present.

Do not sign expiring-credit or expiring-seat terms without the budget owner and Finance
bought in — flag it explicitly in the handoff.

## Renew vs. Reopen a Competitive Process

**Lean toward renew (with negotiation) when all hold:**

- The vendor hit its SLAs across the prior term.
- The renewal ask is flat or a low single-digit increase.
- Switching cost genuinely exceeds several months of contract value and has been quantified
  in writing.
- No credible new entrant has appeared since the last signing.

**Reopen to competition when any hold:**

- A large renewal pairs with a meaningful price increase.
- The vendor missed SLAs across multiple quarters.
- A credible alternative has emerged that the team has not evaluated recently.
- Usage has moved sharply (up or down) from contracted volumes — the old economics no
  longer fit.
- The original deal was sole-sourced with no competitive process.

Size the dollar triggers to Apollo's own thresholds rather than a fixed number here — the
point is the pattern, not the exact cutoff.

**Run a real market check, not a decoy.** Before a significant renewal, get one or two
genuine competing quotes from alternatives you would actually consider. A credible competing
bid routinely takes 10–15% off a renewal ask. Keep it honest: soliciting bids you would
never honor burns supplier relationships and your own credibility. The leverage comes from
alternatives that are real.

**Timeline guidance** (scale to contract size; defaults, not policy):

| Contract size | Start renewal work | Decide on non-renewal notice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Large | ~180 days out | ~90 days out |
| Mid | ~120 days out | ~60–90 days out |
| Small | ~90 days out | Per the contract's notice window |

**On non-renewal notices:** putting non-renewal on the table surfaces a vendor's best
pricing — but a formal notice can carry contractual weight and is not always cleanly
reversible. Before sending anything formal: read the contract's notice mechanics, confirm
whether it can be withdrawn, and loop in Legal. Signal willingness to walk early; reserve a
formal notice for when you have checked the paper.

**Auto-renewal:** do not accept auto-renewal without at least a 60-day cancellation window.
Flag auto-renewal language for removal in negotiation; if it is already in executed paper,
route to `/apollo-legal:vendor-contract-review`.

## Initial Contract vs. Renewal: Leverage Map

Leverage shifts between these two moments. Know which game you are playing.

| Situation | Leverage | Best use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Initial, 2+ competing bids in hand | Maximum | Lock rate, exit rights, rollover, onboarding credits |
| Initial, sole-sourced | Moderate | Shorter initial term; pilot/POC before committing |
| Renewal, well ahead of expiry | High | Market check, price hold, true-down, rollover |
| Renewal, close to expiry | Moderate | Non-renewal signal, scope-expansion trade |
| Renewal, up against the deadline | Low | Accept rate; negotiate only the next term length |
| Auto-renewal already triggered | Very low | Negotiate the next cycle; this one is committed |

- **At initial contract, front-load concessions** — extra seats, onboarding credits,
  implementation support, extended trials. They are cheapest for the vendor at signature and
  hardest to claw back later. Anchor on the competing bid; never give your ceiling first.
- **At renewal, scope is the lever** — consolidating other spend into the contract, or
  committing to a growth trajectory, in exchange for better unit economics.
- **Change the negotiator when renewals have drifted.** If the same owner has run a vendor
  for years and pricing keeps creeping up, put Procurement or Finance in the lead. Vendors
  calibrate to the person across the table.

## Formal Approval Runs Through Zip

Negotiation strategy is only half the job — the deal still has to clear Apollo's formal
process. The multi-year-specific point: build approval lead time into the negotiation
timeline itself, so you do not agree a signature date you cannot hit through approvals. When
terms are agreed, switch to Handoff mode.

See `procurement-handoff.md` for Zip submission and approval routing details (including the
IronClad trigger and parallel-lane mechanics), and route contract redlines to
`/apollo-legal:vendor-contract-review`.

## Quick Reference

Principles, not fixed numbers — the numbers live in the deal:

```
DISCOUNT:    No fixed tier. Anchor, get a number, ask again, trade every give,
             stop when they stop moving. The target is their floor.
TERM:        1-year default. Longer only for proven need + real switching cost
             + a discount and price hold worth locking for. Fast-moving/AI: 1-year.
             Any multi-year keeps an escape valve (opt-out / cap / rollover / refresh).
RAMP-UP:     Default reject; counter flat blended. Accept only if Y1 <= market,
             increases <= CPI/low single digits, usage-based, forecast in writing.
USE-OR-LOSE: Reject; rollover is the floor. Hard expiry -> price the forfeiture in
             and escalate to budget owner + Finance.
RENEW/RFP:   Reopen on a big increase, repeated SLA misses, a real new alternative,
             a sharp usage shift, or an old sole-source. Always run an honest market check.
NOTICE:      Start early (size-dependent). A formal non-renewal notice has weight —
             check the contract and Legal before sending; signal willingness to walk first.
APPROVAL:    Multi-year triggers Apollo's formal Zip/IronClad process — build approval
             lead time into the negotiation. Details in procurement-handoff.md.
LEVERAGE:    Initial -> competing bids + front-loaded concessions.
             Renewal -> start early, scope as the trade. Late -> you've lost the rate round.
```
